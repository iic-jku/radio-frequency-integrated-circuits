In practical RFICs, unwanted tones and unintended coupling paths often limit the achievable dynamic range (expressed for example as SNR or EVM) or cause performance issues (like out-of-band emissions in TX or RX blocking) that render standards-compliant operation impossible. In modern carrier-aggregation transceivers, the integration of several TX and RX paths together with multiple local oscillators on a single die turns this into a major source of concern (Sadjina et al. 2020).

A spur (short for “spurious tone”) is a discrete spectral line at an unwanted frequency. A common metric is the spur level relative to the wanted carrier, expressed in dBc:

Crosstalk is undesired signal transfer from an aggressor node to a victim node through electric, magnetic, substrate, or supply coupling (Verghese et al. 1995). Coupling mainly happens through parasitic capacitances from high voltage-swing nodes, or inductively from high-current loops. Shared traces (like supply and ground connections) can also lead to ohmic coupling.

9.1 Coupling Paths

Coupling Paths

Figure 105 shows the main on-chip coupling paths in a typical TRX architecture. The following discussion uses the crosstalk nomenclature introduced in (Sadjina et al. 2020).

Coupling Paths

  • TX-to-RX coupling through the RFIC, duplexer and shared front-end elements (“TX leakage”) can cause issues such as gain desensitization, reciprocal mixing, receive-band noise from the TX, and IM2/IM3 distortion in the RX (Aparin and Larson 2006).
  • RX VCO coupling (“RX LO leakage”) from the RX VCO/PLL into the LNA/mixer can cause increased IM2 products and LO radiation.
  • LO-to-LO coupling through the LO distribution network can cause reciprocal mixing due to LO spurs in RX, or OOB emissions on TX. When the two oscillators run at nearby frequencies, injection pulling between the VCOs can also degrade the spectral purity of both LOs (Razavi 2004).
  • TX VCO coupling (“TX LO leakage”) from the TX VCO/PLL into the PA can cause increased LO leakage and EVM degradation.

9.2 Synthesizer Spurs

Synthesizer Spurs

  • Reference spurs at integer multiples of the reference frequency, \(f_\mathrm{spur} = f_\mathrm{LO} \pm k\, f_\mathrm{ref}\), \(k \in \mathbb{N}^+\). They are caused by charge-pump mismatch and leakage, finite isolation of the loop-filter node, and supply or substrate coupling at \(f_\mathrm{ref}\). Their amplitude is set by how strongly the loop filter rejects components at \(f_\mathrm{ref}\) relative to the loop bandwidth.
  • Fractional-\(N\) spurs generated by the periodicity and quantization noise of the \(\Delta\Sigma\) modulator that controls the divider (see Section 7.4.1). They typically appear as families of tones around \(f_\mathrm{LO}\) at offsets related to the fractional ratio. Higher-order modulators and dithering reduce their level at the cost of increased close-in noise.
  • Divider and digital-switching spurs at sub-harmonics of \(f_\mathrm{LO}\) and at digital clock frequencies, coupling into the VCO through the supply and substrate.
  • Digital spurs originating from TDC or DCO nonlinearities in all-digital PLLs, or from digital blocks in the vicinity of the VCO.

Mitigation of spurs and crosstalk is a complex topic that requires careful design of the entire system, including the architecture, floorplan, circuit design, layout, and packaging. Some common techniques are summarized here. In addition, inductor-to-inductor coupling has to be considered and may require quadrupole inductors (Poon et al. 2009) or patterned ground shields (Yue and Wong 1998).

Substrate and supply coupling deserve special attention because they couple every block to every other block through a common low-impedance node (Verghese et al. 1995). Effective measures include placing sensitive analog and RF blocks in dedicated deep-n-well or triple-well pockets, surrounding aggressors and victims with substrate guard rings, separating supply domains for digital, analog, RF, and LO blocks, and using on-chip and on-package decoupling so that switching currents do not modulate the supply seen by the VCO or LNA. A careful floorplan that keeps high-swing TX nodes, LO distribution, and digital clocks physically far from sensitive RX inputs and VCO inductors is usually more effective than any post-layout fix.

If supply decoupling is insufficient, series or shunt supply regulators and additional low-pass filters for supply and reference lines can help to reduce supply-bound coupling (El-Nozahi et al. 2010). However, these measures increase the power consumption and area of the RFIC, and may not be feasible in all applications.

Where possible, mitigation should also be performed by digital signal processing of the RX or TX baseband signals, for example digital predistortion, digital IM2 cancellation (Gebhard et al. 2019), or digital self-interference cancellation (Sadjina et al. 2020). However, these techniques require additional hardware and power, and are not always feasible.
